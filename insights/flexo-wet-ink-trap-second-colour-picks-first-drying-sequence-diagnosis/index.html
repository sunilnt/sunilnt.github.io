Flexographic Wet-Ink Trapping and Interstation Drying

Second Flexo Colour Pulls or Mottles the First? Test Wet-Ink Trap Before Changing Anilox Volume

Diagnose poor wet-ink trapping by checking the overprint target, colour sequence, first-down ink film, interstation drying, viscosity and press speed in a controlled order.

20 November 20267 min readRoyal Coat

A two-colour flexo overprint can look clean at setup speed and become weak, mottled or dirty when the press reaches production speed. The second colour may fail to transfer evenly, or it may disturb the ink film already on the substrate. That is a wet-ink trapping problem until the evidence shows otherwise. Do not begin by fitting a higher-volume anilox or adding impression. First compare the first colour alone, the second colour alone and the overprint under the same press condition. The comparison separates an ink-film, drying or sequence problem from a mechanical print fault.

Define the failed overprint, not only the final colour

Mark a representative repeat and record substrate, treatment side, ink system, colour sequence, anilox IDs, plate and tape package, dryer settings, speed and the point at which the appearance changes. Print and retain three patches: first colour only, second colour only and the two-colour overprint. Measure or assess them with the same approved method and lighting. A normal single colour with a poor overprint points towards trapping; a weak single colour must be corrected before the overprint can be judged.

Check whether the second colour refuses the first, picks it, forms a grainy film or changes only in high-coverage areas. Also note whether the fault follows one station, one ink batch, one substrate roll or one speed range. Flexographic Technical Association guidance identifies print sequence, ink drying rate, pH or viscosity, dryer settings and press speed as variables that can change wet-ink trap. Record those conditions before making several adjustments together.

Prove the first-down ink film before adjusting the second

Run the first colour alone at crawl, setup and normal production speed. Check laydown, density or colour target, surface condition and the plant's approved dry or cure response before the web reaches the next station. The objective is not to dry the film completely unless the process specification requires it. It is to put the surface in the qualified condition for the next ink to transfer without lifting, mixing or repelling.

On film, there is no absorbent fibre network to take up the liquid carrier. FTA guidance notes that drying must occur from the printed surface and that premature surface skinning can retain solvent beneath it. Use the ink supplier's approved formulation and drying window. A surface that feels dry by hand can still be outside the process requirement, while excessive interstation heat can move the web or ink away from the qualified condition.

Check viscosity, temperature and circulation together

Sample both inks after circulation has stabilised and again when the trap fault appears. Record viscosity with the maintained plant method, ink temperature, pH where relevant, time, station and press speed. Do not correct a viscosity number without checking temperature and the approved make-up material. Two samples can give different flow readings because their temperatures differ, even when the operator has not deliberately changed the ink.

InkSpec states that its sensors measure both viscosity and temperature, and that automatic control should allow mixing time after a controlled fluid addition. That sequence matters in any control method. A fast correction followed by an immediate second addition can create a swing that changes transfer and drying. Confirm that the return line is circulating the complete ink volume and that additions are mixed before accepting the next reading.

Treat anilox volume as part of an ink-film balance

Confirm the installed anilox identity, measured condition and cleanliness at both stations. Compare the current certificate or maintained volume record with the approved job baseline. Zecher explains that actual transfer volume depends not only on cell geometry but also on surface condition, fluid rheology, surface tension and the doctoring process. The nominal engraving on the job sheet is therefore not proof of the ink film being transferred today.

A larger volume can strengthen a single colour and still make wet trapping worse if the first film exceeds the available interstation drying capacity. FTA process guidance recommends evaluating the full transfer and drying system rather than using maximum ink volume as the target. Use the lowest controlled film that meets the colour and coverage requirement, then prove that the next colour transfers cleanly at normal speed.

Run a short sequence-and-speed trial

When the artwork and colour requirements allow it, compare the present sequence with a controlled alternative. Keep the substrate, ink batches, aniloxes, impression and measurement method unchanged. If the fault follows the first-down ink rather than a physical station, review its formulation, film weight and drying requirement. If it stays with one station, inspect that station's airflow, temperature control, anilox, doctoring and impression before changing the colour order permanently.

Test from setup speed through the normal operating range and include a planned slowdown and restart. Record the point at which the overprint changes, not only the final accepted sample. Inspect for colour contamination, picking, mottle, pinholing, dirty highlights, set-off and retained solvent or odour where the approved packaging procedure requires it. A result accepted only at reduced speed is a temporary operating limit, not evidence that the production process is stable.

Release the job with a saved trapping standard

Save the accepted first colour, second colour and overprint patches with the sequence, substrate, anilox IDs, ink batches, viscosity, temperature, pH where relevant, dryer settings, speed and measurement results. Keep the failed samples as well. They give the next shift a practical boundary and let the ink, anilox or drying supplier see the same fault rather than working from a general complaint about colour variation.

Questions from production and purchasing teams

What to ask about flexographic wet-ink trapping and interstation drying

What does poor wet-ink trapping look like in flexo printing?

The overprinted colour may appear weak, grainy, mottled or contaminated, or the second plate may disturb the first-down ink. Compare each colour alone with the overprint before classifying the fault.

Should I increase anilox volume when the overprint looks weak?

Not before checking the first-down film and interstation drying. More wet ink can exceed the available drying capacity and make trapping worse even if the single colour becomes stronger.

Why does wet-ink trap fail only at production speed?

Higher speed changes dwell time, airflow, ink behaviour and the interval available between colours. Record the speed at which the overprint changes and inspect the complete transfer and drying route.

Can changing the colour sequence fix poor trapping?

It can change the result, but use a controlled trial. If the fault follows one first-down ink, investigate its film, formulation and drying requirement instead of treating sequence alone as the cure.

What records should be saved for a repeat flexo job?

Save single-colour and overprint samples plus substrate, sequence, anilox IDs, ink batches, viscosity, temperature, relevant pH, dryer settings, speed and the approved measurement results.
