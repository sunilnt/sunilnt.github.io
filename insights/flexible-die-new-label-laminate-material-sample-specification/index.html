Flexible Die Fails After a Laminate Change? Specify the Material Again

Specify a flexible die for a changed label laminate by sending the exact face, adhesive and liner, then confirming blade geometry, cylinder details and cut tests.

15 September 20267 min readRoyal Coat

A flexible die can cut one paper laminate cleanly and strike through the liner when the artwork moves to PP or PET. The label shape has not changed, so the old tool is often treated as suitable. The cutting system sees a different job: face material, adhesive, liner thickness, surface treatment and required release can all affect the result. Before adding pressure or reordering the same die, give the tooling supplier the complete laminate and a production sample.

Prove that the problem began with the material change

Keep accepted samples from the old laminate and failed samples from the new one. Mark the machine direction, operator side, die repeat and position of every incomplete cut or liner strike. Record the material codes, batches, face, adhesive, liner, press speed, die, magnetic cylinder, anvil and pressure setting.

Run the approved old construction again only if material and production conditions allow a safe controlled comparison. If the original laminate cuts correctly with the same tooling and the fault returns with the new construction, the material change is useful evidence. If both fail, inspect the die, cylinder, anvil and station before blaming the laminate.

Measure the new face and liner at several positions using the maintained method. Do not work only from nominal thickness or a supplier family name. Two constructions sold for a similar label application can require different cutting geometry or depth.

Send the complete laminate, not a generic description

Identify the face stock, coating, adhesive and release liner by manufacturer, product code and batch where available. State whether the label is paper, PP, PE, PET, Tyvek or another synthetic, and whether the adhesive is permanent, removable, high-tack or otherwise application-specific. Include any varnish, laminate or printed layer that reaches the cutting station.

Wilson Manufacturing advises sending the actual production material to the die maker because blade angle, coating, clearance and depth depend on the construction being cut. A small sample from the approved roll is more useful than a data sheet alone because the die can be tested against the material that will run on press.

Send enough web for handling and test cuts, with the unwind direction, print side and liner side clearly marked. If more than one approved material may run under the same stock code, provide each version and say whether one die must cover them all. The supplier then has to evaluate that range rather than test the easiest sample.

Let the material determine the cutting edge

Kisscut publishes different cutting-angle examples for different laminates: around 80 degrees for paper, 70 degrees for standard paper and PP, 60 degrees for synthetic materials such as PE, PET and Tyvek, and sharper options for strong adhesives or difficult constructions. These are manufacturer examples, not settings to copy onto an order without review.

A keener edge can enter a film or difficult adhesive differently from a broader paper-oriented edge. The suitable geometry also depends on die height, cylinder diameter, flexible-die thickness, liner and the strike required. Ask the die maker to record the selected geometry against the material sample and application.

Do not ask the press operator to recover the wrong edge geometry with more station load. Excess pressure can mark or weaken the liner and increase wear while the difficult corner or adhesive area still fails. Preserve the failed sample so the tooling supplier can see where the specification needs attention.

Confirm the cylinder, anvil and die height together

Provide the press make and model, die-station details, tooth count or repeat, magnetic-cylinder drawing, undercut or drop, anvil specification and running direction. Kisscut states that die height is selected from the application and cylinder diameter. A laminate change review is incomplete when the supplier sees the material but not the installed cutting system.

Inspect the magnetic-cylinder surface and the back of the flexible die before mounting. Remove contamination by the approved method and check for corrosion, dents, a kink or a raised edge. Confirm the anvil condition, bearings, gear and bearer contact. A local mechanical fault can appear at the same time as a material change and produce a misleading diagnosis.

Set the station using the press and tooling procedure and begin with the least load that produces the approved cut. If more pressure is needed on one side or at one repeat position, measure the pattern and inspect the component at that location. Do not save uneven compensation as the new material recipe without finding its cause.

Select coating and life options from the actual run

Give the die maker the normal run length, repeat frequency, number of planned reruns, press speed, cleaning method and whether the face, ink or coating is abrasive. A short paper job and a repeated film job may justify different steel, edge treatment or surface coating even when the outline is identical.

Kisscut offers standard hardened-steel flexible dies, laser-hardened edges and its I-NOV abrasion-resistant option. Wilson lists standard and extended-life flexible dies for film, with optional non-stick surface treatments. Ask what problem each option addresses on the supplied laminate instead of choosing the longest-life name by default.

A treatment cannot correct an incorrect die height, wrong liner information or poor station condition. Agree on the acceptance test first: complete face cuts, protected liner, stable matrix stripping, clean corners and acceptable dispensing or downstream performance. Tool life should be considered only after the die produces that result.

Test the new specification and keep the evidence

Test the die with the intended laminate from setup through normal production speed. Check the full web width and complete repeat, including small radii, sharp corners, narrow gaps and areas with heavy adhesive exposure. Inspect the release liner under suitable light and magnification, then test matrix stripping and dispensing where they form part of the job.

Record the accepted material and batch, die ID and drawing revision, edge or treatment option, magnetic cylinder, anvil, starting load, warm running condition, speed and approved sample. If another laminate is later approved, repeat the comparison rather than adding its code to the old record without a cut test.

Questions from production and purchasing teams

What to ask about flexible die specification

Can the same flexible die cut paper and film versions of one label?

Do not assume it can. The face, adhesive, liner and suitable cutting edge may differ. Send both production laminates to the die maker and ask for written confirmation and test results.

Why should an actual laminate sample be sent with the order?

The die maker can test the construction that will run on press and select blade geometry, clearance, coating and depth from the real face, adhesive and liner rather than nominal data alone.

Should extra pressure be used when a new film does not cut?

First confirm the material, die specification, cylinder, anvil and station condition. Excess pressure can damage the liner and tool while hiding the real mismatch.

Does a long-life treatment correct the wrong blade angle?

No. Edge hardening or a surface coating addresses wear or release conditions. The die geometry and height still have to suit the laminate and cutting system.

What should be approved during the die trial?

Approve complete face cutting across the web and repeat, liner condition, matrix stripping, finished-label dispensing where relevant, production speed and a retained sample linked to the exact tooling and material.
