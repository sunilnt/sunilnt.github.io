Gauge Bands in Slitting and Rewinding

Hard Bands Build Across a Rewound Film Roll? Map Caliper Before Increasing Tension

Diagnose hard bands in rewound printed film by mapping cross-web caliper, print coverage, roll build, tension and lay-on pressure in the right order.

10 November 20266 min readRoyal Coat

A printed film or laminate may leave the slitter with one or more hard raised bands across the roll face while the adjoining areas remain softer. Increasing rewind tension can make the outside look tighter, but it may also compress the same thick lane more heavily through every wrap. First establish whether the band follows the printed or coated design, the parent roll or one winding position. Then adjust tension and lay-on pressure only after the cross-web thickness pattern is understood.

Map the band against the printed and coated areas

Mark operator side and drive side on the parent roll and on each finished roll. Measure the location and width of every hard band from the same web edge. Compare those positions with heavy ink coverage, opaque white, varnish, adhesive or an unprinted lane. If the raised area follows the artwork or coating map across several repeats, investigate cumulative caliper before changing the slitter-rewinder.

FTA's slitter-rewinder guide explains that even a small thickness increase in printed regions is magnified as layers accumulate on a growing roll. The resulting cross-web bands are commonly called gauge bands. The same guide warns that web tension and lay-on nip load can promote permanent deformation in those local regions once they are wound.

Keep one accepted roll and one failed roll from the same job. Record finished diameter, slit width, core, speed and the point at which the band became visible.

Separate incoming caliper variation from the winding position

Inspect the parent roll face before slitting. Look for the same raised stripe, baggy lane, edge build, telescoping or a local area that is already wound harder. If the pattern exists before the web enters the machine, preserve that evidence. A rewind correction may reduce the visible symptom, but it cannot remove a thickness profile created during printing, coating or lamination.

Compare lanes when the job is slit. If the same design zone produces a band regardless of where it winds, the incoming web or artwork layout deserves priority. If the problem stays at one rewind position while different lanes pass through it, inspect that shaft position, core fit, differential element where fitted, separator clearance and lay-on contact.

Check the core before altering the recipe. Record its material, inside diameter, wall condition, width, roundness and end squareness using the plant's approved method. A damaged or out-of-round core can create a local roll-build problem that resembles a tension fault.

Do not use extra tension to crush a thick lane flat

Return to the last approved rewind-tension and taper settings before testing. Compare the failed job with a stable job on the same machine and substrate family, but do not copy a setting simply because the nominal film thickness matches. Printed coverage, coating, laminate construction, coefficient of friction, roll width and finished diameter can all change the winding condition.

Make small controlled trials and label every roll. If increasing tension makes the hard band firmer while the adjoining area remains unchanged or becomes distorted, stop the trial. The winding system is loading a cross-web thickness difference rather than correcting it. Check the trial again after unwinding because permanent deformation or bagginess may be more obvious in the next process than on the completed roll face.

Treat line speed as a test variable, not the automatic remedy. Air can enter between layers at higher speed, particularly with low-friction materials. FTA notes that lay-on rollers can be required for low-COF webs to reduce trapped air and improve roll build. More nip is not a universal answer.

Match lay-on and rewind control to the finished-roll range

One common lay-on roller may work for uniform rolls but may not load several slit packages equally when their caliper or diameter develops differently. FTA's guidance notes that individual lay-on rollers are often required where caliper variation needs separate nip control. Define the narrowest and widest slit, roll spacing, material range and changeover frequency before specifying the arrangement.

PRATI's published digital-finishing configurator lists automatic rewind-tension control across shaft sizes, a pneumatic lay-on roller, selectable tension modes for pressure-sensitive labels, unsupported film and clear-on-clear work, and pneumatic or differential shaft options. These are configuration choices, not a substitute for an application brief. The job mix determines which controls need to act independently and which settings must be saved.

For a machine review, use real problem material. Run the printed or coated design that creates the band, not only a uniform unprinted web. Test acceleration, normal speed, deceleration and the full target diameter. Unwind the roll through the next process before accepting the configuration.

Build a repeatable trial record before changing artwork or equipment

Record the substrate or laminate construction, nominal and measured caliper, print and coating coverage map, parent-roll position, slit layout, core specification, shaft type, finished diameter, speed, tension profile, taper and lay-on setting. Add photographs of the roll face and the web after unwinding. This shows whether the fault follows the design, the material or the machine position.

Where artwork creates a persistent heavy lane, involve prepress and the customer before redistributing coverage or changing the layout. A design change must still meet colour, opacity, barrier, appearance and regulatory requirements. Where the material varies, use the supplier's quality route with marked samples and measured positions rather than a general complaint about winding.

For a Royal Coat review, send the press or coating route, material construction, web and slit widths, artwork coverage map, parent and finished-roll diameters, core details, shaft and lay-on arrangement, speed, tension record and one accepted and one failed sample. Those details allow the cross-web thickness and winding controls to be reviewed together.

Questions from production and purchasing teams

What to ask about gauge bands in slitting and rewinding

What is a gauge band in a rewound roll?

It is a raised or harder cross-web band created when a locally thicker region builds through many wraps. Printed, coated or laminated coverage can contribute to the thickness difference.

Should rewind tension be increased to remove a hard band?

Not before mapping the band against the web and design. Extra tension can compress the thicker lane more heavily and may create deformation that appears when the roll is unwound.

How can I tell whether the fault comes from the web or one rewind position?

Mark the web edges and compare several lanes and rolls. A band that follows the same design or parent-roll position points towards incoming caliper; one that stays at one rewind position points towards local winding hardware or setup.

When is an individual lay-on roller useful?

It can be useful when slit packages need separate nip control because their widths, diameters or caliper profiles differ. Confirm the need with representative production material and the machine supplier.

What should be sent for a gauge-band application review?

Send the material construction, measured caliper map, artwork coverage, web and slit layout, core and roll dimensions, shaft and lay-on arrangement, speed, tension profile and marked accepted and failed samples.
