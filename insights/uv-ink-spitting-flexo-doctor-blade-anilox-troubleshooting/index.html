UV Ink Spitting in Flexo: Check the Doctor Blade Before Changing the Anilox

Find the cause of UV ink spitting on a flexo label press by checking the print pattern, doctor-blade contact, ink condition and anilox engraving.

25 November 20266 min readRoyal Coat

The first pull looks clean. As the label press picks up speed, spots of one colour start appearing in a clear margin or across a reverse. They are not in the artwork and do not land at the same point on every repeat. UV ink spitting is one possible cause: ink passes the metering blade and reaches the web where it is not wanted. Before ordering a different anilox or raising chamber pressure, find out where the excess ink comes from and whether the blade holds a clean wipe at production speed.

Use the printed web to identify the suspect station

Save pulls from before the first marks, at the speed where they appear and after returning to the earlier speed. Mark the machine direction, time, colour and cross-web position. Include a clean background or reverse if the job has one. A dark spot or short irregular mark in the same colour as one station is a useful clue. A pale hole in a solid is missing ink, and calls for a different investigation.

Lay several repeats beside each other. If the mark recurs at the same image position, inspect the plate and related rotating parts. A continuous line in one lane calls for a check of the blade edge, contamination and the anilox surface. Scattered marks that increase as a UV deck speeds up make spitting worth investigating, but the pattern alone does not prove it. If the press procedure allows an isolated deck pull, use it to confirm which colour introduces the marks.

Inspect the working edge before increasing blade pressure

Use the press isolation procedure before opening the chamber or touching tooling. Photograph any ink accumulated past the metering edge and identify which blade it is on. Ink at a chamber end or dripping from the containment blade is a separate observation. It does not, by itself, show that ink passed the metering edge and printed on the web.

Check the blade part number and orientation, the clamp seat and the contact pattern along the used edge. Look for damage, dried deposits under the clamp and uneven wear across the blade. Confirm that the chamber sits parallel to the anilox and that the seals fit the chamber and roller. If one end needs extra force to seat, correct that condition rather than loading both ends harder.

The blade specialists writing in FLEXO describe two ways the setup can work against the operator: viscous ink can deflect an inadequately supported edge, and too much applied load can flatten the contact angle and let ink past. Begin with the press maker's procedure and the lowest load that gives a clean wipe and stable containment. Ask the blade supplier to review blade body stiffness and tip profile together; a stiffer body does not mean the working edge has to be blunt.

Check ink condition at the point where the fault starts

Keep the ink product, batch, mixing method and temperature with the press record. Compare the readings at the first clean pull and at the first defective one; if there was a refill or a long stop between them, log it. Some UV inks change their flow behaviour with mixing and shear. A sample from a container that has been standing should not be treated as equivalent to ink from the running deck.

Use the measurement method specified for that UV ink. A flow cup used for water-based or solvent-based jobs may not be suitable for the same comparison. Do not add solvent or a reducer from another ink system to see whether the spots disappear. If the ink supplier recommends an adjustment or a different grade, record that change and recheck colour, cure and the required resistance or adhesion tests.

Trial an engraving against the same print target

First verify the installed roller's ID, measured volume where available, line count, engraving and condition. If the press previously ran the same ink and roller cleanly at that speed, find out what changed before spending on another roller. Give the anilox supplier the blade and chamber details along with the failed pull.

Zecher documents conventional 60-degree and open-cell ceramic engraving options. Where an engraving trial is justified, compare candidates that can meet the same ink laydown requirement. Keep the press, ink batch, plate, substrate and blade setup consistent where possible. Record any unavoidable difference, such as a change in measured volume, so it is clear what the trial did and did not isolate. A roller that removes the spots by weakening a solid or losing white opacity has not met the job specification.

Approve the run at speed, including a restart

Set a review area, such as an unprinted strip next to the artwork or a reverse where spots are easy to see. Compare equal lengths of web at setup speed, normal speed and after a planned restart. Use the approved visual or camera inspection method and retain the first failing pull alongside the passing one. Verify colour, details in screens and reverses, and the required cure and adhesion after the metering change.

For a Royal Coat application review, send the press and deck, ink product and batch, ink temperature at the onset, blade profile and chamber settings, anilox certificate, substrate, running speed and labelled pulls showing the first marks. A full-width sample with the running direction marked is more useful than a close-up alone.

Questions from production and purchasing teams

What to ask about uv flexo ink spitting and doctor-blade setup

Why does UV ink spitting start only at higher press speed?

Speed can change how ink passes the blade and how the edge holds contact. Confirm the onset on sequential pulls and compare blade setup and ink temperature before attributing the fault to speed alone.

Will higher chamber pressure stop UV ink spitting?

It can make the wipe worse if the blade bends and its working angle flattens. Check seating, alignment and the approved blade setup before increasing load.

Can an anilox change solve UV spitting?

Engraving is a valid trial variable once blade setup and ink condition are controlled. Compare the printed defect rate and the required colour or opacity together.
