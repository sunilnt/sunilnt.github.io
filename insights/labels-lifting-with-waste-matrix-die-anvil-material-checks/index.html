Label Lifting During Matrix Stripping

Labels Lifting With the Waste Matrix? Check Whether the Fault Follows the Die, Anvil or Material

Diagnose labels lifting with the waste matrix by mapping the fault, checking the die and anvil, measuring the liner and reviewing the stripping path.

8 September 20267 min readRoyal Coat

A label that leaves the liner with the waste matrix has already passed through the cutting nip. Raising die pressure may make the next few metres run, but it can also mark the liner and load the tooling without correcting the cause. Keep the failed web and map the pattern first. The position and frequency of the lifted labels can show whether the fault follows one die cavity, stays on one side of the machine, changes with the material or begins only when the matrix reaches the stripping point.

Map the failed labels before touching the pressure

Stop with enough web intact to see the die-cut and stripping result together. Mark the machine side, direction of travel, die repeat and the point where the label began to lift. Keep samples from before the fault, the failed section and the first acceptable section after any correction.

Count the interval between failures. If one label lifts at the same cavity on every die revolution, inspect that part of the tool. If the fault appears at a length unrelated to the die repeat, include the anvil and machine rotation in the check. If a complete side of the web is affected, look for a cross-machine condition rather than treating every cavity as damaged.

Wilson Manufacturing's die-cutting guidance uses this pattern-based approach. A repeating fault can separate a local cutting-edge problem from an anvil or press condition. Record the pattern before changing pressure, speed, stripping angle or waste tension, otherwise the first useful evidence is lost.

Check whether the fault follows the die

Clean the flexible die and magnetic cylinder using the approved method, then inspect the affected cavity under good light. Look for a damaged or low cutting edge, contamination below the die, a lifted corner, corrosion, adhesive residue or a local mark. Confirm that the die was made for the present face, adhesive and liner construction.

Where the machine and tooling procedure permits it, reversing a flexible die can be a useful controlled test. If the fault moves with the same area of the die, concentrate on that tool. If it remains on the same side of the machine, return the die to its approved orientation and inspect the anvil, bearer condition, parallelism and loading on that side.

Do not keep increasing force to make a damaged edge cut. Wilson notes that excessive force can flex a tool and make the centre cut differently from the sides. A die that carries a confirmed low or damaged area needs assessment or replacement, not a permanent pressure correction.

Check the anvil and cross-web loading

Inspect the anvil surface, bearers, journal blocks and the maintained cutting gap. Look for wear, dents, contamination or a band that repeats independently of the die cavity. If an approved serviceable anvil is available, a controlled comparison can show whether the fault remains with the original anvil.

Labels lifting along one machine side can indicate that the tool is not parallel with the anvil. Before making an adjustment, check that the die station is clean, correctly seated and loaded according to the manufacturer's procedure. More pressure on both sides can hide a local alignment issue while increasing load on the good side.

Mark any temporary gap or pressure correction in the job record. An adjustable or stepped anvil may help complete a controlled run when the tooling and material assessment supports it, but it should not conceal wear or a die that was made for a different liner. Plan the permanent repair or correct tool specification separately.

Measure the material that is actually on press

Confirm the face material, adhesive, liner type and batch against the tool order. Measure liner and laminate thickness with the maintained method and compare several positions across the roll. A small material change can appear as random lifting, while a larger or systematic change may affect every repeat.

Inspect the cut edge and adhesive behaviour. An aggressive adhesive can string into the matrix, while a removable or low-tack construction may allow the label to travel with the waste more easily. Check whether the label dispenses cleanly from a retained sample and whether the liner carries a damaging die strike.

Send representative material with the tooling enquiry. Wilson's troubleshooting notes repeatedly connect die performance with the material used when the tool was produced. A familiar product name is not enough if the liner, adhesive coat or face construction has changed.

Change the stripping path only after the cut is sound

Once the cut and liner condition are acceptable, watch where the matrix separates. Check the stripping angle, distance from the die, roller diameter, web support and waste-rewind response. The label needs to remain supported on the liner while the waste changes direction. A long unsupported path or sudden tension change can pull a marginal shape away.

Wilson's stripping guidance shows that a smaller stripping roller can increase the separation angle when labels follow the waste. It also describes stripping close to the die and using cavity foam for difficult shapes. Apply these methods only through the approved tooling and machine procedure. Incorrect foam, position or pressure can create another cutting or stripping problem.

Change one item and run through acceleration, normal speed and deceleration. A setup that works only while the machine crawls is not a production correction. Watch the narrowest waste bridges and the lifted-label area together so an improvement in one does not create matrix breaks somewhere else.

Prove the result and save the evidence

Inspect the liner, label edge and waste from the corrected run. Confirm that labels remain on the liner, the matrix stays intact and the finished roll will dispense as required. Check more than one die revolution and include the normal speed and finished-roll tension in the trial.

Save the substrate and batch, face, adhesive and liner details, measured thickness, die and anvil IDs, fault position, repeat interval, press side, speed, pressure or gap setting, stripping arrangement and photographs of the cut and waste. Record whether the fault followed a reversed die, stayed with the machine side or changed with another verified material roll.

Questions from production and purchasing teams

What to ask about label lifting during matrix stripping

Why do labels lift with the waste matrix?

The cause may be a low or damaged cutting edge, anvil wear, uneven loading, liner variation, adhesive behaviour or an unsuitable stripping path. Map the fault pattern before changing pressure.

What does it mean if the same die cavity lifts every repeat?

Inspect that cavity and the surface beneath it for damage, contamination, a low cutting edge or poor seating. A fault that repeats with the tool is more likely to belong to the die than to a random material change.

Why are labels lifting only on one side of the web?

Check whether the fault follows the die when an approved reversal test is possible. If it stays on the same machine side, inspect anvil condition, parallelism, journal blocks, seating and cross-web loading.

Should I increase waste-rewind tension when labels follow the matrix?

Not as the first response. Verify the cut, liner and adhesive first, then inspect the stripping angle, support and waste path. Excess waste tension can break narrow matrix bridges.

What should be sent with a die-cutting support enquiry?

Provide the die drawing, material and liner specification, measured thickness, press, die and anvil IDs, speed and settings, retained web and matrix samples, and photographs showing the fault pattern and stripping path.
