Static Control in Converting

Static Control in Film Slitting and Rewinding: Find Where the Charge Is Generated

Map static generation through treatment, slitting, rollers and rewinding, then place maintained neutralisation where film converters need it.

15 August 20268 min readRoyal Coat

Static on a film line is not one problem at one location. Corona treatment, separation, slitting, path rollers and rewinding can each generate or redistribute charge. A bar fitted far from the web or hidden behind a grounded roller may do little. Map the process, measure under representative conditions and control charge close to the point where it affects handling, contamination or operator safety.

Treat shocks as a safety signal

Follow the plant's electrical and hazardous-area rules. Static can shock operators and, where flammable vapours or dust exist, can contribute to ignition risk. A production guide cannot replace a competent site risk assessment.

Ground machine frames and conductive components as designed and inspect bonding. Do not assume an ioniser corrects a missing earth.

Map generation points

Measure or observe charge after corona treatment, unwind, nip separation, slitting, idlers and rewind. Record web side, polarity, distance, speed, humidity and material. Charge can change with a new film, coating or ambient condition.

Meech notes that corona treatment, slitting, separation, path rollers and winding all affect static on film lines. The control plan may need more than one location.

Place neutralisation close to the web

Ionising equipment has an effective working distance and must see the charged surface. Install it according to the maker's distance, angle, speed and grounding guidance, with safe access for cleaning.

After slitting, separate lanes may carry different charge. Treating before separation alone may not control what is regenerated downstream.

Protect inspection and roll build

Static attracts dust and can cause film to cling, wrinkle, misguide or wind poorly. Inspect whether nuisance alarms or contamination increase with charge. Avoid increasing web tension to overcome electrostatic sticking.

Measure both before and after the control point while the line is at normal speed and roll diameter.

Maintain bars, power supplies and sensors

Clean emitter pins or surfaces at the specified interval and inspect cables, alarms and power supplies. Contamination reduces ionisation performance. Record cleaning and measured residual charge rather than marking the bar 'working' because its indicator is on.

If a bar is moved for threading or maintenance, verify its position before production.

Revalidate when the construction changes

A different resin, coating, laminate, speed or humidity can change charge behaviour. Include static checks in trials for new films and high-speed jobs.

Store the material, conditions, measurement points and approved equipment settings with the job. The useful result is controlled handling and a safe process, not merely fewer visible sparks.

Run a controlled production trial

Use high-charge film at maximum normal speed, low humidity and the largest rewind diameter. Agree the test roll, normal operating range and responsible operator before starting. Begin from verified mechanical condition and approved consumables. Change one variable at a time, allow the process to stabilise and retain samples from make-ready, steady production and the end of the roll. A short demonstration at an easy setting does not prove that the process will hold through a commercial job.

During the trial, monitor grounding, treatment, slitting, separation, roller contact, ioniser position, distance and cleanliness. Mark every deliberate change and production event on the same timeline. Acceptance should require residual charge inside the plant limit, stable handling, reduced contamination and no operator shocks. If the result is outside the agreed limit, preserve the sample and machine state long enough to identify the cause instead of making several unrecorded corrections.

Turn the result into a pressroom standard

Save material, humidity, speed, measurement points, polarity, charge before and after control, and maintenance. Attach photographs, measurement reports and an approved finished sample where useful. Give the recipe a revision and link it to the exact material, artwork or machine configuration. Operators should be able to see what may be adjusted, the permitted range, the time allowed for stabilisation and who approves an exception.

Define a stop and escalation rule for a shock, hazardous-area limit, failed ground, ioniser alarm or charge that cannot be controlled safely. Review repeat faults by station, material and shift instead of treating each one as a separate surprise. A good standard does not remove judgement; it gives experienced operators reliable evidence and prevents the next shift from undoing a successful correction.

Questions from production and purchasing teams

What to ask about static control in converting

Can one static bar control an entire converting line?

Sometimes not. Charge can be regenerated after treatment, slitting, separation and winding, so map the full path.

Does an ioniser replace grounding?

No. Grounding of conductive components and active neutralisation perform different functions.

Why does static return after slitting?

Web separation and subsequent roller contact can generate new charges downstream of the first control point.

What should be changed first during the trial?

Start from verified mechanical condition and approved consumables, then change only one controlled variable at a time.

What makes the result repeatable on the next shift?

A revision-controlled recipe with component identity, settings, measurements, approved samples and clear stop rules.
