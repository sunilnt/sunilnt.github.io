Ink Set-Off and Backside Transfer

Ink Transfers to the Back of a Rewound Film? Prove Drying or Cure Before Changing Rewind Tension

Diagnose ink set-off inside a rewound film roll by checking the defect pattern, ink laydown, drying or cure, press speed and roll-build conditions in the right order.

29 October 20266 min readRoyal Coat

A printed film can look acceptable at the press and show ink on the reverse side only when the roll is unwound at inspection, lamination or pouch making. That is set-off or backside transfer, and it should not be treated first as a rewind-tension problem. The ink or coating may have entered the roll without enough drying or cure for the pressure and temperature inside the finished roll. Preserve the evidence, prove the printed layer is ready to rewind and then check how the roll was built.

Map the transferred image before changing the press

Keep the first affected roll intact long enough to identify the contact pattern. Mark the unwind direction, printed side, operator side, drive side, roll number and the distance from the core or outside diameter where the fault begins. Photograph the printed image and the reverse-side transfer together. If the transferred mark matches a specific colour, coating or heavy solid, record that deck rather than describing the whole roll as wet.

Check whether the fault is continuous, appears after a speed increase, follows a stoppage or is concentrated near the core. Compare it with the last accepted roll from the same job. A continuous image transfer points the investigation toward ink-film condition and winding contact. A short band may relate to a press stop, dryer recovery, UV restart or an operating change. Preserve time, speed and roll-change records so the symptom can be placed back on the press run.

Do not wipe the transferred surface before taking a sample. Label a section from the accepted roll and one from the failed roll, then store them as required by the plant's ink, solvent and food-packaging procedures. The first useful question is whether the printed layer was ready for face-to-back contact when it entered the rewind.

Prove drying or cure under the actual production condition

Film is non-porous, so the carrier in a water-based or solvent-based ink cannot disappear into the substrate. Flexographic Technical Association guidance explains that drying must occur from the ink surface and that trapped solvent can harm adhesion, physical properties and odour and can affect later converting such as lamination. A dry-looking surface is not sufficient evidence if solvent or water remains below a skin formed on the ink film.

Use the approved test for the chemistry and end use. That may include a rub, tape or blocking check, a retained-sample comparison, residual-solvent testing or another method agreed with the ink, coating and packaging specification. For UV or LED UV material, confirm the specified ink or coating, source spectrum, dose or irradiance measurement method and the required finished-product cure test. Do not substitute one generic fingernail or touch test for the qualified release method.

Repeat the test at the actual production speed, ink laydown, web temperature and source-to-substrate condition. A sample passed during a slow setup does not prove that the dryer or curing system has capacity at normal speed. Record the result at startup, after acceleration and after a representative production interval.

Hold laydown, ink condition and drying capacity together

FTA guidance on anilox configuration warns that a press can apply more ink or coating than it can dry or cure. It specifically connects insufficient drying or cure with material being wound into a roll and transferring to the substrate's backside. Before adding dryer power or slowing the press, confirm the anilox identity and condition, blade and chamber setup, ink or coating batch, viscosity or pH where relevant, temperature and the intended colour, opacity or coat-weight target.

A higher-volume anilox, an uncontrolled viscosity change or a second hit of white can increase the wet film that the dryer or curing unit must handle. FTA also notes that variation in ink drying time can produce smearing and other print faults. Restore the qualified ink condition and laydown before using extra heat or lamp output to compensate for an unintended deposit.

Then inspect the drying or curing path using the equipment supplier's procedure. Check that the correct units are enabled, airflow or cooling is available, filters and optical components are in their maintained condition and the web follows the qualified path. For thermal drying, confirm the approved temperature and airflow records. For UV or LED UV, compare a controlled measurement with the established baseline using the same instrument, spectrum, distance, position and speed.

Check roll build after the printed layer passes

Once drying or cure is demonstrated, review rewind tension, taper, nip, core condition, alignment, roll temperature and finished diameter. Excessive contact pressure can make a marginal ink film fail sooner, while a loose or unstable roll introduces different defects. Rewind settings must suit the substrate, thickness, roll diameter and next process; they should not be changed simply to hide ink that is not ready for winding.

Compare roll hardness across the width and through the build using the plant's maintained method. Look for a fault that is worse near the core, at one edge or after a diameter change. Confirm that the core and chuck did not slip and that the web was not wound hot. If only one lane or edge is affected, relate it back to cross-web laydown, drying or cooling before making a global tension change.

Make one controlled correction and produce a separately identified trial roll. Allow the roll to stand for the same time and under the same conditions that exposed the original defect, then unwind it through the normal inspection or downstream process. Release production only when the printed face, reverse side, adhesion, rub or cure result, odour or residual-solvent requirement where applicable, roll build and downstream test all pass together.

For a Royal Coat review, send the substrate and construction, ink or coating system, anilox and press details, speed, dryer or curing setup, rewind settings, finished diameter, time before inspection and labelled samples showing both the original image and the reverse-side transfer. Those records separate an ink-film problem from a roll-build problem before settings or equipment are changed.

Questions from production and purchasing teams

What to ask about ink set-off and backside transfer

What is ink set-off in a rewound roll?

It is transfer from the printed or coated face onto the reverse side of the adjacent web layer while the material is in roll contact.

Should rewind tension be reduced first when set-off appears?

No. First prove that the ink or coating is adequately dried or cured at the actual production speed and laydown. Then review the qualified tension, taper and nip settings.

Why is film more sensitive to incomplete drying than paper?

Film is non-porous, so the liquid carrier cannot absorb into the substrate and must leave through the printed surface.

Can a higher anilox volume contribute to backside transfer?

Yes. A heavier ink or coating deposit can exceed the available drying or curing capacity, especially when speed and the rest of the process remain unchanged.

How should a corrected roll be approved?

Identify it separately, hold it for the relevant time and condition, unwind it through the normal inspection or downstream process and confirm both product-performance and roll-build requirements.
