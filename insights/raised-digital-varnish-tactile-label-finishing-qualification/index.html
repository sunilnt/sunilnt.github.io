Raised Digital Varnish and Tactile Label Finishing

Raised Digital Varnish Looks Good but Fails After Rewinding? Qualify the Finished Roll

Qualify raised digital varnish and tactile label effects by controlling artwork, coating, UV cure, inspection, slitting, rewinding and finished-roll tests.

13 November 20267 min readRoyal Coat

A raised varnish can look sharp at the printhead and still reach the customer with flattened detail, blocking, scuffing or poor registration. The coating has to survive curing, web contact, inspection, slitting, rewinding, storage and application. Treat the tactile effect as a finished-label requirement, not only a decorative file. Define its height, position, cure and resistance before the job is released, then prove those conditions on material taken from the completed roll.

Define what the tactile feature must do

Mark the approved artwork revision and identify every raised area, knockout, fine line and critical clearance from text, barcode, die-cut edge and label gap. State whether the feature is a visual gloss contrast, a texture, a tactile mark or a functional element. A general instruction such as 'high-build varnish' does not tell production what result must be measured or rejected.

Agree the acceptance checks before setup. These can include registered position, visible appearance under controlled light, coating height or laydown, surface tack, gloss, rub or scratch resistance and adhesion to the printed construction. Add the customer's handling or application test where it is known. The first attractive sample at the machine is not enough if the wound roll will be stored, transported or dispensed under pressure.

Lock the incoming print and finishing file

Check the printed roll against the finishing data before coating. Confirm web width, repeat, unwind direction, lane count, eye mark, substrate, ink or toner system and surface treatment. Compare the varnish file with the actual print revision at a magnified control point. A small artwork mismatch can look like unstable register even when the web and finishing unit are running correctly.

Keep a printed but uncoated reference and mark operator side, drive side and machine direction. Record the roll and batch, conditioning time and any overprint or protective layer already present. If the same file behaves differently on another material or print route, investigate wetting, adhesion and cure on the complete construction instead of copying the previous recipe unchanged.

Build the effect with the actual process route

PRATI describes digital finishing configurations that can combine digital varnishing and embellishment with lamination, cold foil, die-cutting, inspection, slitting and rewinding. Write the exact route for the job and identify where the tactile layer is applied. A varnish that is later laminated, foiled or pressed through a nip sees a different process from a final exposed surface.

Set up on the production substrate and chemistry. Record the coating or varnish code, batch, printhead or coating unit, temperature where controlled, laydown setting, web speed and curing recipe. Change one variable at a time and retain labelled strips from each trial. Do not increase coating and curing power together, because the team will not know which change produced the result.

Prove cure with measurements and a physical test

Match the UV or LED system to the approved varnish chemistry and substrate. IST Metz explains that UV irradiance is the peak intensity and UV dose depends on exposure time and therefore web speed. Record both with the maintained measuring method where the plant uses one. A power percentage on the control panel is a recipe value, not a direct measurement of the energy reaching the coating.

Use the release tests agreed for the product. IST Metz lists coating characterisation that includes surface topography, coating thickness, gloss and mechanical testing. On press, use the plant's approved practical checks for tack, rub, scratch, adhesion and downstream resistance, then confirm any laboratory or customer-specific method required for the application. Stop the job if the surface looks cured but transfers, marks or changes after handling.

Inspect the effect without creating false rejects

Set inspection zones around the raised feature, adjacent print, codes and die-cut clearance. A reflective or textured surface can change the camera image as lighting and web angle change. Establish an approved master from a fully cured sample and test the defect sensitivity at setup and production speed. Do not reduce sensitivity across the whole label merely to quiet one decorative zone.

Check cross-web and repeat-to-repeat consistency. Measure a representative solid area, a fine feature and the most critical registration point. If one lane differs, examine coating delivery, printhead condition, web flatness and curing across the width before applying a common correction. Keep the rejected sample and its machine position so maintenance can relate the mark to the physical system.

Protect the surface through slitting and rewinding

Trace every point where the coated face contacts a roller, guide, nip or wound layer. Look for gloss change, scuffing, offset, blocking or flattened texture after those contacts. Confirm knife setup and dust control so debris does not become trapped against the raised surface. A label that passes immediately after curing must also pass after the full web path.

Build a trial roll at the intended width, core, diameter, tension, taper and lay-on condition. Allow it to stand for the agreed time, then unwind material from the outside, middle and near the core. Compare the tactile relief, appearance, release and surface resistance. If the effect changes deeper in the roll, review cure and winding pressure together rather than judging only the first few metres.

Release the job from the completed roll

The release record should link the artwork and finishing-file revision, substrate, print route, varnish, laydown setting, curing system, irradiance and dose readings where used, speed, inspection master, slit format and rewind recipe. Attach the accepted sample and record where it was taken from the roll. This gives the next shift a reproducible standard rather than a photograph of a decorative effect.

Questions from production and purchasing teams

What to ask about raised digital varnish and tactile label finishing

Why can raised varnish look acceptable before rewinding but fail in the roll?

The surface may be insufficiently cured or may be compressed, scuffed or transferred by web contact and winding pressure. Check samples from through the finished roll, not only at the coating unit.

Is the curing-unit power percentage enough to approve the job?

No. It is a machine recipe value. Use the maintained UV measurement method where applicable and confirm the agreed physical and end-use tests on the actual coating and substrate.

Should inspection sensitivity be reduced for a reflective tactile effect?

Not across the whole label. Build controlled zones and an approved master, then verify that critical text, codes, register and surface defects remain detectable.

What should be checked after the trial roll stands?

Unwind samples from the outside, middle and near the core and compare relief, gloss, tack, scuffing, transfer, blocking, adhesion and the required application test.

What information should be sent for a raised-varnish application review?

Send the substrate and print construction, artwork revision, varnish, desired effect, speed, curing data, web path, slit and rewind format, accepted and failed samples and the final product test.
