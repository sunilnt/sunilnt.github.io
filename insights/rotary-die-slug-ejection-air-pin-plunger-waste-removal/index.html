Rotary Die Slug Ejection

Die-Cut Slugs Staying in the Cavity? Match the Ejection Method to the Waste

Diagnose rotary die slug-retention faults by checking the cut, waste path and capture method before selecting air, pins, plungers or vacuum.

16 October 20267 min readRoyal Coat

A small cutout that remains in a rotary die cavity can reappear on the web, damage the cutting edge or travel into the finished roll. This is different from a matrix that breaks during stripping. The retained piece is an individual slug, and the useful starting point is to prove that it has been cut cleanly before changing the ejection system. Preserve the faulty sample, mark the cavity and record where the loose waste is finally found. That evidence helps separate an incomplete cut from a cleanly cut slug that is not being removed or contained.

Define the slug and mark the point of failure

Keep the die number, machine, station, job, material construction, width, speed, tension and approved pressure setting. Record the slug shape and dimensions, its position across and around the repeat, and whether every repeat or only an occasional repeat is affected. Retain examples of the cut web and any loose waste recovered downstream.

Follow the waste through one controlled machine cycle. A slug may stay inside the cavity, remain partly attached to the web, transfer to the anvil, land on the printed surface or reach the rewind. Mark the first confirmed location. Do not rely on the finished roll alone, because the piece may already have passed through several nips before it becomes visible.

Inspect the cavity and cutting edge under the approved stopped and isolated condition. Waste packed inside the tool can load the blade and contribute to damage. Do not pick at the cavity with a knife or metal tool. Secure the equipment, use the tooling maker's cleaning method and quarantine a die with a chipped, cracked or damaged cutting edge.

Prove the cut before changing the ejection method

Inspect the cut edge around the complete slug. Check whether the top material has separated cleanly and whether the liner or backing has the intended condition for the job. A hanging fibre, adhesive bridge or uncut corner points to a cutting problem. More ejection force will not correct a slug that is still attached to the web.

Review die-to-anvil condition, bearer cleanliness, pressure records, material caliper and the approved setup. Compare the affected cavity with an accepted cavity on the same tool when possible. A local fault that follows one cavity should not trigger a general pressure increase across the station. Extra load can mark the liner, accelerate tool wear or hide the original evidence.

Repeat a short, controlled test after the cut condition is corrected. If the slug separates but remains in the cavity or travels unpredictably, the issue has moved from cutting to removal. Record that distinction for the tooling review instead of reporting that the die simply does not cut.

Check whether air ejection suits the actual tool and plant air

An air-eject die directs compressed air through the tool to release cut waste. Wilson Manufacturing lists rotary-union, probe and manifold arrangements, and notes that proper web paths and air pressure matter. These are engineered configurations. Do not drill an existing tool, alter a journal or connect an improvised air line on the machine.

Record the plant air supply available at the machine under production demand, the connection arrangement, filtration and maintenance condition, and the location at which the slug must leave the cavity. A pressure reading away from the tool may not describe the air available during the ejection event. The required design should come from the tool maker using the die, cavity and machine details.

Inspect hoses, rotary connections, probes, manifolds and guards by the approved isolated procedure. Look for leaks, damage, contamination and a web path that returns the released slug to the product. Restoring airflow is incomplete unless the waste also clears the web and enters a controlled collection route.

Compare mechanical ejection when compressed air is unsuitable

Wilson's pin-eject design removes slugs without an external air source. The published arrangement keeps the pin recessed while the die cuts against the anvil. After the cavity passes the anvil, a rubber roller core returns the pin and expels the slug. The cavity geometry, blade clearance, machine arrangement and waste path still need engineering review before the method is specified.

Spring-plunger dies also provide mechanical ejection without compressed air. Wilson describes spring-loaded plungers that compress during cutting and release afterwards, helping prevent waste buildup inside the cavity. Pin and plunger systems are not interchangeable labels. Each has dimensional, material and operating requirements that must be checked against the actual cutout.

Send a dimensioned drawing rather than a verbal description such as small hole. Include the slug length, width, shape, corner radii, repeat, cross-web position, material layers and thickness, adhesive condition, grain or fibre direction where relevant, and the clearance around nearby blades. This lets the tool maker assess whether a mechanical element can be placed and supported safely.

Design the capture route as part of slug removal

Ejecting the piece from the cavity does not guarantee that it leaves the process. The slug needs a controlled route away from the web, anvil, guards and finished roll. Observe the release point at the approved test speed and confirm where the waste travels. Stop if loose pieces can enter another station or contaminate the product.

Wilson describes a scrap-recovery attachment that mounts on the exit side of the die station and connects to a vacuum source to catch ejected waste. It also includes an anvil scraper to discourage waste from collecting on the anvil. A proposed attachment must be checked against the machine frames, available space, guarding, vacuum capacity, web path and access for cleaning.

For a vacuum tool that carries waste through the die, confirm the slug size and the internal exit path with the tool maker. Do not assume that an existing plant vacuum or general cleaning vacuum is suitable for continuous process use. Collection, disposal, noise, dust and maintenance requirements belong in the equipment review before production trials begin.

Release the job only after full-speed waste control is proven

Test the agreed correction with the actual production construction. Include startup, acceleration, normal speed, a planned stop and restart, and enough repeats to challenge every relevant cavity. Inspect the cut, liner or backing, anvil, die cavities, web surface, waste collector and finished roll. A clean cavity is not sufficient if slugs remain on the product.

Record the accepted die and ejection configuration, air or vacuum condition where applicable, operating settings, collection method, inspection frequency and cleaning interval. Add photographs of an accepted slug and web. If one material grade needs a different arrangement, keep that distinction in the job record rather than treating the setting as universal.

Questions from production and purchasing teams

What to ask about rotary die slug ejection

Is a retained slug the same as a matrix-stripping fault?

No. Matrix stripping removes the surrounding waste web. A retained slug is an individual cutout that remains attached, stays in a die cavity or travels loose through the process. Record which condition is present.

Should die-cutting pressure be increased when slugs stay in the cavity?

Only after the cut has been inspected and the approved setup reviewed. If the slug is already cut cleanly, more pressure does not solve the removal path and can increase wear or liner damage.

What is the difference between pin eject and spring plunger dies?

Both can eject waste without compressed air, but their mechanisms and dimensional requirements differ. Pin eject uses pins returned by a rubber roller core; spring plungers compress during cutting and release afterwards.

Why are loose slugs still appearing after air ejection works?

The slug may be leaving the cavity without entering a controlled collection route. Check the release point, web path, anvil, guarding and the suitability of the vacuum or recovery system.

What information should be sent for a slug-ejection review?

Send the machine and die station, die drawing, slug dimensions and position, material construction and thickness, cut requirement, production speed, air and vacuum details, photographs, samples and the downstream location where waste appears.
