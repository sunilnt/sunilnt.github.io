UV Curing Unit Running Hot or Tripping? Check Cooling Airflow Before Raising Lamp Power

Diagnose a UV curing unit that runs hot or trips by checking alarms, cooling airflow, filters, exhaust, optics and cure results in a controlled order.

11 October 20267 min readRoyal Coat

A UV curing unit that runs hotter than normal, alarms during a long job or trips after the press reaches speed does not automatically need more lamp power. The problem may be restricted cooling air, a loaded filter, an exhaust fault, contamination inside the unit or a maintenance condition that changes as heat builds. Increasing the output can add more heat without restoring the cooling path. Preserve the alarm and production condition, let the system reach a safe state using the manufacturer's procedure, then check the cooling and curing sides separately.

Preserve the exact condition at the first alarm

Record the curing-unit ID, station, alarm text or code, time, lamp or LED power setting, web speed and how long the unit had been running. Add the ink, varnish or adhesive, substrate, web width and the finished-product test that was being used. A note that says 'UV overheating' is too broad to separate an air-flow problem from an optical or electrical fault.

Note whether the event occurred during warm-up, acceleration, steady production, a slow-down or a stopped-web condition. Compare the affected station with the other units on the same press. If only one station moves away from its normal temperature or airflow, retain that difference as evidence instead of changing every station to match it.

Do not bypass a temperature switch, airflow switch, cover interlock or alarm so that the job can continue. Stop and isolate the equipment under the press and UV-system instructions before opening guards or removing filters. The fault record is useful only if the team handles the equipment safely and does not erase the condition with several unrecorded adjustments.

Compare cooling behaviour with a known normal run

Use the values and indicators available on the installed system. Compare fan status, air-flow indication, exhaust condition, temperature trend, cooling-water information where fitted and the time taken to reach the alarm. The exact checks depend on the unit. Do not apply a temperature or airflow target from a different model without the maker's documentation.

Inspect the route from the cooling-air intake to the exhaust. Look for blocked grilles, collapsed or disconnected ducting, closed dampers, accumulated dust and anything recently stored against an intake. Confirm that fans start when expected and that maintenance covers and seals are correctly fitted. A clean component will not solve the problem if the overall path is restricted.

Ask what changed before the first event. Building work, seasonal dust, an altered exhaust route, a replaced filter, a new enclosure or a press move can change the cooling condition. Keep the date of that change beside the alarm history. A fault that appears only after longer runs often needs a trend, not one cold inspection at the start of a shift.

Inspect dust and activated-carbon filters on schedule

IST METZ states that dust filter mats protect the cooling system and should be checked monthly for contamination and replaced when necessary. Treat monthly as the formal inspection point, then shorten the local interval when the pressroom has more dust or the cooling-air composition requires it. Record the unit, filter position, date, condition and replacement part.

A filter should be identified before it is removed. Confirm its type, size, airflow direction and whether it is a dust mat or an activated-carbon element. IST METZ explains that activated-carbon filters, where present, capture odours and help break down ozone. Reduced effectiveness, including developing odours, is a replacement signal described by the manufacturer.

Do not judge a filter only from the clean-looking side. Compare both faces, the frame, seating and surrounding grille. Check that the replacement is fully seated and that covers close correctly. If the filter blocks unusually quickly, investigate the contamination source rather than accepting frequent replacement as the permanent solution.

Separate a cooling fault from lost UV output

Temperature and cure are linked in the unit but they are not the same measurement. Restricted cooling can create alarms, while dirty lamps, reflectors or quartz surfaces can reduce the UV radiation reaching the web. Raising lamp power may hide a reduced-output condition briefly and increase the thermal load at the same time.

Inspect optical parts only by the approved service method. IST METZ notes that dust can accumulate on lamps and reflectors, burn onto hot surfaces and reduce radiation efficiency. The manufacturer also warns against touching reflector surfaces with bare hands because contamination can burn in. Use the specified protective equipment, cleaning materials and replacement procedure.

After the cooling path is restored, compare UV dose or irradiance with the established baseline using the correct instrument and geometry. Keep source power, speed, measuring position and warm-up condition with the result. A temperature reading cannot prove cure, and a radiometer value cannot confirm that the cooling system is healthy.

Prove the repair under production load

Restart only after the responsible person has released the system. Begin with the approved warm-up and observe fan, airflow, exhaust and temperature behaviour before feeding production. Then bring the press to the normal job condition in controlled steps. Record the same values that were captured at the first alarm so the comparison is meaningful.

Run long enough to reach the point at which the event previously occurred. A five-minute test does not prove a repair when the unit tripped after an hour. Watch all stations, the electrical cabinet and the exhaust route. If the temperature continues to rise or the alarm returns, stop and escalate with the preserved records rather than increasing the output again.

Approve the printed or coated web separately. Check the plant's cure, adhesion, rub, blocking or product-specific release test on marked samples. Compare the result with the approved job baseline. The maintenance work is complete only when the system remains stable and the finished construction passes its release requirement.

Build the cooling system into preventive maintenance

Keep one record for filter inspection, fan and exhaust checks, cleaning, alarm history, lamp or LED service and cure measurements. Trend the findings by unit rather than storing isolated maintenance slips. Repeated filter loading, rising temperature or falling UV output then becomes visible before the press stops during a production job.

IST METZ describes its UV-system service check as including exhaust air and cooling, pneumatic and filter components, together with visual inspection of lamps and reflectors. Use that scope as a prompt when reviewing an ageing system, but follow the installed unit's manual and service plan for the actual tasks, limits and authorised personnel.

Questions from production and purchasing teams

What to ask about uv curing cooling system maintenance

Should lamp power be increased when a UV curing unit runs hot?

Not before the cooling path and alarm cause are checked. More output can add heat without correcting a loaded filter, restricted intake, exhaust fault or fan problem.

How often should UV-system dust filters be checked?

IST METZ advises checking dust filter mats monthly and replacing them when necessary. The local interval may need to be shorter where dust or cooling-air conditions are more severe.

Can a clean filter prove that cooling airflow is correct?

No. The intake, grille, fan, duct, damper, exhaust route, covers and seals also need to be checked according to the system instructions.

Why check UV output after cooling maintenance?

Cooling stability does not prove that the required UV radiation reaches the web. Compare dose or irradiance with the controlled baseline and repeat the finished-product cure test.

What information should be sent for a recurring UV overtemperature fault?

Send the unit and press IDs, alarm code, timing, power, speed, temperature or airflow trend, filter and exhaust history, maintenance work, photographs, chemistry, substrate and cure result.
